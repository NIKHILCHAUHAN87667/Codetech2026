SAT-SA · SUPERVISORY ANALYTICS TOOL FOR SOC ASSESSMENT

Analytics Methodology & Data SchemaNCIIPC — Problem Statement 26157

SAT-SA helps NCIIPC supervisors identify which Critical Sector Entities, processes and cases warrant closer review — by turning periodic SOC operational evidence into explainable, evidence-backed supervisory signals. It supports human examiners; it does not replace their judgement.

01The analytics approach

A metric is a measurement. It is not, by itself, a supervisory finding. SAT-SA moves from raw evidence to a human decision through a deliberately staged chain — each stage adds context before anything is called a signal.

Why core and supporting metrics are kept separate

Every metric in this specification belongs to one of two classes. The split exists so that a supervisory finding is never generated by a single thin or context-only measurement acting alone — which matters directly for explainability and for validating SAT-SA against expert manual review.

Core metrics

May, on their own or in combination, produce a candidate execution-gap or negative-space signal.

Supporting / contextual metrics

Never trigger a finding alone. They corroborate, weight or explain a core signal — e.g. distinguishing capacity strain from disregard.

02Core supervisory metrics

Eight metrics. Each is defined with its formula, the data it requires, how to read it, and a worked (illustrative) example. Every core metric is tagged by the kind of signal it can contribute to.

OSECObserved Security Evidence Coveragenegative space

Proportion of assets expected to produce security evidence that have at least one qualifying evidence record in the assessment period.

OSEC = (assets with expected evidence observed ÷ assets expected to produce evidence) × 100
Example

40 assets expected to log evidence; 34 have a qualifying record → OSEC = 34/40 × 100 = 85%

Reading it

Low OSEC flags a possible visibility gap — it does not by itself prove monitoring is absent.

CAMGCritical-Asset Monitoring Gapnegative space

Proportion of critical assets expected to be monitored that show no qualifying evidence in the period.

CAMG = (critical assets without qualifying evidence ÷ critical assets expected to be monitored) × 100
Example

12 critical assets; 2 show no evidence → CAMG = 2/12 × 100 ≈ 16.7%

Reading it

High CAMG marks a critical-asset blind spot worth review — a negative-space candidate, not a confirmed failure.

TTCPTime-to-Close Percentileexecution gap

Where a case's time-to-close falls within a comparable population (same CSE, alert type, severity and period).

TTC = closed_at − opened_at TTCP = PercentileRank(TTC | comparable group)
Example

A critical-alert case closes in 2 hours, while comparable cases typically take much longer → case lands near the 8th percentile (unusually fast).

Reading it

Unusually low TTCP should always be read together with ICS — speed alone is not evidence of quality.

ICSInvestigation Completeness Scoreexecution gap

How many of the applicable investigation requirements are actually evidenced in the record (alert reviewed, evidence examined, findings documented, escalation decision documented, etc.).

ICS = [ Σ(wⱼ × Iⱼ) ÷ Σwⱼ ] × 100, Iⱼ ∈ {0,1}, wⱼ = weight of requirement j
Example

6 of 8 equally-weighted requirements satisfied → ICS = 6/8 × 100 = 75%

Reading it

Low ICS means fewer expected investigation elements are evidenced in what was submitted.

EREscalation Compliance Rateexecution gap

Of the cases that meet defined escalation criteria, how many were actually escalated.

ER = (qualifying cases actually escalated ÷ cases meeting escalation criteria) × 100
Example

10 cases qualify for escalation; 6 were escalated → ER = 60%

Reading it

The denominator is qualifying cases only — not all cases handled in the period.

PRRPost-Remediation Recurrence Rateexecution gap

Of the conditions recorded as remediated, how many recur — same asset, same/equivalent condition — within a configured window.

PRR = (conditions recurring after recorded remediation ÷ conditions with recorded remediation) × 100
Example

25 conditions remediated; 4 recur within the window → PRR = 16%

Reading it

High PRR identifies a recurring condition — it does not automatically prove the remediation failed.

CEMRClosure-Evidence Mismatch Rateexecution gap

Of cases closed as resolved or remediated, how many lack the supporting evidence the submission would be expected to contain.

CEMR = (resolved/remediated cases lacking supporting evidence ÷ resolved/remediated cases) × 100
Example

50 cases closed as remediated; 9 lack supporting evidence → CEMR = 18%

Reading it

Required wording: "No supporting evidence was found in the submitted dataset." Never phrase this as proof remediation didn't occur.

KECGKPI–Evidence Consistency Gapexecution gap · governance

The discrepancy between what a CSE reports for a KPI and the value SAT-SA independently derives from the same evidence — only once both are aligned on definition, population, period and scope.

Dₖ = |ReportedKPIₖ − ObservedKPIₖ| KECG = Σ(wₖ × Dₖ) ÷ Σwₖ
Example

Reported closure-rate KPI = 95%; SAT-SA-observed = 81% → Dₖ = 14, contributing to a composite KECG once combined with other KPIs.

Reading it

High KECG means reported performance materially differs from what the submitted evidence shows — not proof of manipulation.

03Supporting / contextual metrics

These never generate a finding on their own. They corroborate a core signal, adjust its priority, or supply the context needed to interpret it correctly.

04Composite prioritisation — CEGS

The Composite Execution-Gap Score exists to rank the worklist, not to issue a verdict on SOC quality.

CEGSₑ = Σ(wₖ × Sₑ,ₖ) — Sₑ,ₖ = normalised strength of signal k for entity e; wₖ = configurable weight

Inputs may include monitoring, investigation, escalation, recurrence, closure-evidence and KPI-evidence signals. Weights live in SAT-SA's configuration — never in the CSE's own submission. A higher CEGS means higher review priority; it is not a definitive compliance judgement.

05Signal generation

Signal = f(Metric, HistoricalBaseline, PeerBaseline, Threshold, Context)

In sequence: metric → sample sufficiency → historical/peer baseline → deviation → configurable rule → persistence/trend → contextual check → signal → evidence → human review.

SignalCondition
Potential superficial investigationunusually low TTCP + low ICS, with sufficient sample
Potential monitoring blind spotlow OSEC + high CAMG, with historical/peer deviation
KPI–evidence discrepancyelevated KECG, with aligned definitions and sufficient evidence
Post-remediation recurrenceelevated PRR, sufficient recurrence sample, persistent trend

Data sufficiency is not the same as poor performance. "ICS is low despite sufficient investigation records" is a performance signal. "ICS cannot be reliably assessed because investigation records are missing" is a data-sufficiency limitation. SAT-SA must always distinguish the two before a finding is raised.

06Data schema

The CSE submits underlying operational evidence only. SAT-SA independently derives every metric, baseline, signal, explanation and priority score — nothing analytical is taken from the CSE at face value.

07Extending the model

This methodology and schema are intentionally non-exhaustive. The eight core metrics, five supporting metrics and nine schema entities documented here cover the data environment described in the problem statement — they are a starting set, not a ceiling.

New metrics are added as configuration — a formula, its required fields, a signal-type tag and an explanation template — through SAT-SA's metric registry, without changing the Analysis Engine, the Signaling Module, or any other core processing logic. New schema entities or fields extend the same way: additive, versioned, and validated before promotion to active use. The system is designed to grow with NCIIPC's supervisory understanding, not to be re-engineered every time it does.
